A knowledge base is a bet on when you pay for the thinking

This post sorts nine repositories I had filed under one heading. The useful distinction turns out to be timing. It matters when the system makes sense of your documents, and what a mistake looks like afterwards. Three of the nine were misfiled and aren’t knowledge bases at all.

My notes have a heading called “RAG & Document Intelligence”, where RAG is retrieval-augmented generation. I have filed nine repositories under it. Read back in one sitting, they are not the same kind of machine. One embeds screenshots. One parses PDFs. One compiles a wiki. One is a Datalog reasoner. One is an Airtable clone.

The label no longer told me what I would be installing, so I went looking for a question that would.

It isn’t “graph or vector”. LightRAG and Semantica both build a knowledge graph, and I can’t think of a situation where one substitutes for the other.

The question that works is about timing: when you pay for the thinking, and what being wrong looks like afterwards. Every one of these tools makes sense of a pile of documents. They differ in when that happens, and each choice fails in its own way.

Pay at query time

Classic retrieval. You index the corpus, and the understanding happens when someone asks.

Nothing accumulates. Ask the same question twice and it is answered from scratch twice. The index is derived and opaque. There is no version of it you would want to read.

This is the cheapest option to stand up, and the only one that never gets smarter. It also fails in the most forgiving way. A bad chunk comes back, you notice, you rephrase. The failure is loud and it is over in a minute.

Pay at build time

Here something reads everything once and writes down its conclusions. The output is a document you could read with the tool uninstalled.

OpenKB is the clearest example. Running openkb compile turns a document pile into a cross-referenced wiki. Its README states the split plainly:

Traditional RAG rediscovers knowledge from scratch on every query. Nothing accumulates. OpenKB compiles knowledge once into a persistent wiki, then keeps it current.

My own vault belongs in this group, without the compiler. The synthesis step is me, or an agent I am supervising.

What you buy is that the hard part happens once, and everything downstream reads the result. What you pay took me longer to see. Compilation is a commitment. Something decided what the summary says. If two sources disagreed, something decided how to present that.

So the failure mode inverts. In retrieval, a bad answer is visibly a bad answer. In a compiled wiki, a bad synthesis becomes the record, and it is formatted exactly like a good one. It fails quietly, and it keeps failing, because it looks settled.

That trade is not a small one. You have moved from “sometimes retrieves the wrong paragraph” to “confidently wrong in writing, indefinitely”.

Pay before you start

The third group inverts it again. You declare the shape of your domain first, then check everything you ingest against it.

Semantica does this with real symbolic machinery. It ships a Datalog reasoner, validation against Shapes Constraint Language (SHACL) ontologies, and an explanation generator that returns the derivation steps. You can ask it why and get a proof rather than a paragraph.

Utopia applies the same instinct at enterprise scale. Its documentation is explicit that it is neither a knowledge graph nor a vector store. Ontology and time awareness are implemented in the base layer, conflict detection runs against that ontology, and there is a built-in audit trail. It deploys offline on hardware you control.

This is the most expensive of the three to adopt, and the only one that survives a reader who does not trust you. Its failure mode arrives earliest and costs the most. If you modelled the domain wrong, you find out after ingestion, and the fix is to model it again.

Pick by the failure you can live with, not by the architecture that sounds current.

Three of my nine weren’t knowledge bases

The sorting also caught my own filing error, which is the part I would rather not have found.

OpenDataLoader PDF turns PDFs into structured data. DataTrove filters and deduplicates large text datasets. Baserow is a no-code database holding records you typed in.

None of the three makes sense of a corpus. Two are feedstock, one is storage. They ended up under the heading because they arrived during the same project, which is how a useful word turns into a useless one.

One diagnosis deserves stating plainly: sometimes you are missing a parser, not a knowledge base. That fix is cheap and boring, and it resolves a fair share of what gets diagnosed as a knowledge problem.

Where the other four land

LightRAG and PixelRAG both pay at query time. LightRAG reports 39.4k stars and an EMNLP 2025 paper. It will also run all four of its stores on a single Postgres, which is the detail likely to decide a deployment.

PixelRAG skips parsing altogether. It renders pages to screenshot tiles and embeds those. Its argument is that parsing is itself the lossy step, which holds up when your documents carry meaning in their layout.

Semantica and Utopia, covered above, are the two that ask you to pay first.

One caveat

My last two posts here were tests. This one is not. Everything above comes from documentation and source, and each figure I quote is what the project claimed on the day I filed it. This is a map, not a result.

What I want to test next

The line I would most like to put under a test is OpenKB’s:

Cross-references already exist, contradictions are flagged, and synthesis reflects everything consumed.

Contradiction flagging is what build-time synthesis has to get right to justify its compile step. Suppose it cannot tell you that document C contradicts document A. The wiki is then a well-formatted average of your sources, which by the failure mode above is worse than no synthesis at all.

That is testable the way I tested pdf-inspector. Plant the contradictions on purpose, so the right answer is known before the tool runs. Include a near-contradiction as a false-positive control. Whether it catches them is a fact, not an opinion.
